02Architecture

Cloud‑free video, and the thin control plane nobody mentions

Your reasoning for peer‑to‑peer is correct: cloud video storage is the line item that scales with your unit count and never stops growing. We are not proposing to change that. Video and audio go directly between the camera and the phone, and never land on a server we operate.

Worth being precise about

“No cloud” describes the media path, not the whole system. Every peer‑to‑peer camera platform in the market — including the one in your product today — runs an always‑on control plane. Something has to translate a camera's UID into its current address, help two devices behind home routers find each other, and wake a phone whose app has been killed. A vendor who tells you the server count is zero has either not built one of these before, or is quietly planning to keep using someone else's.

The honest description of what we would build is a cloud‑free media path with a thin, India‑hosted control plane. That control plane is small, cheap, and — critically for your mandate — entirely under your control.

03Options

Three different projects are hiding behind one request

Today you buy a complete solution from Shenzhen: silicon, firmware, the peer‑to‑peer stack and the app, bundled. “Replace the app” can therefore mean three very different things, at three very different prices — and only two of them satisfy a mandate about infrastructure.

Our recommendation

Track B, with Track C as the destination once your own board is running. Track B moves device identity, signalling and updates onto Indian infrastructure you own, while continuing to use the existing module — so you keep selling through the transition rather than stopping the line.

Track B has one hard dependency: access to the module vendor's SDK, or firmware source. With it, adapting the firmware to point at your servers is routine integration work. Without it, the same outcome requires reverse engineering, and the estimate in section 06 does not hold. Establishing which of those two worlds we are in is the single purpose of Phase 0.

04Security

Your assembly line is a security advantage

Budget peer‑to‑peer cameras have a well‑documented reputation: shared default passwords, unauthenticated protocols, predictable device identifiers, unsigned firmware. If your mandate is about securing the product, the app is only where the problem becomes visible — the fixes live in how each unit is provisioned.

Because you are bringing PCB assembly in‑house, you gain something the bundled vendor solution cannot offer: the ability to burn a unique key pair into every unit at flashing time. That single change is what makes the rest possible.

  • Per‑unit identity, no shared secrets. Every camera authenticates with its own key. No default password to publish, no credential that compromises the fleet.
  • Encryption from camera to phone. Media protected end‑to‑end, so the relay in section 02 forwards ciphertext and your infrastructure is never a wiretap point.
  • Signed firmware and secure OTA. A unit refuses an image that is not signed by your key — the practical defence against fleet‑wide compromise.
  • No secrets in the app binary. Anything shipped inside an APK is public; the design assumes it will be extracted.
  • Data residency. Control plane in an Indian region, with the DPDP Act's minimisation principle easy to argue when video never leaves the two endpoints.
Please confirm

We have assumed your mandate relates to security requirements for surveillance camera equipment, alongside the usual BIS and WPC routes. Certification timelines and lab queues drive the launch date more than development does, so knowing the exact notification and your compliance deadline changes how we sequence Phases 2 and 3.

  • Phase 0 — Discovery.
